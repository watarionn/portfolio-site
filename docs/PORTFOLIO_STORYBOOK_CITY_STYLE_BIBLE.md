# Portfolio Storybook City Style Bible

Updated: 2026-09-30  
Status: Active visual-production standard  
Scope: Portfolio City World Map, District Views, project architecture, environmental art

## 0. Identity

Formal style name:

# **Portfolio Storybook City Style**

Short definition:

> 欧州絵本都市を基礎に、制御された不均一さ・経年色・情報量の強弱・先を隠す探索構図で作る架空都市画。

English shorthand:

> **European Storybook × Controlled Irregularity × Aged Color × Selective Detail × Exploratory Composition**

The city is not a reconstruction of one real country, city, period, or architectural school.

It is a fictional European-flavored world whose visual coherence matters more than strict historical consistency.

---

## 1. Core visual goal

Portfolio City should feel:

- illustrated rather than rendered
- crafted rather than procedurally perfect
- bright but not glossy
- old but not sepia
- welcoming but slightly quiet
- readable but not over-explained
- coherent but never mechanically uniform
- explorable rather than fully exposed

The city itself should feel like a character.

Roads, gaps, bridges, stairs, roofs, trees, walls, and distant landmarks should suggest that the world continues beyond the current frame.

---

## 2. World model

The visual world may freely combine:

- stone
- plaster
- brick
- timber
- tile
- metal
- civic architecture
- workshop architecture
- waterside structures
- observatory structures
- narrow lanes
- bridges
- courtyards
- terraces
- woodland
- cliffs
- hills
- coastal edges

Do not enforce rigid historical purity.

Prefer:

> "this feels believable inside Portfolio City"

over:

> "this is perfectly correct for one real-world date and region"

The world must remain internally coherent even when its influences are mixed.

---

## 3. Controlled irregularity

A defining rule is **controlled irregularity**.

Recommended mental ratio:

- about 70% shared visual grammar
- about 30% individual variation

Shared grammar creates one world.

Variation prevents the world from looking copied, modular, or procedurally stamped.

### Use irregularity in

- roof heights
- window spacing
- wall angles
- chimneys
- eaves
- steps
- building widths
- street widths
- tree shapes
- retaining walls
- fences
- facade alignment

### Do not use irregularity as random distortion

Avoid:

- every wall leaning
- arbitrary perspective errors
- random broken geometry
- chaotic roof silhouettes
- fake "handmade" wobble on every edge

Irregularity must improve identity, age, and place memory.

---

## 4. Building silhouette

Every important building should be recognizable by silhouette before detail.

Strong silhouette tools:

- roof shape
- dome
- tower
- chimney family
- entrance form
- awning
- projecting bay
- bridge attachment
- terrace
- balcony
- facade step-back
- asymmetrical annex

Avoid relying on text labels to make a building recognizable.

Project-specific identity should primarily come from:

1. silhouette
2. massing
3. architectural motif
4. accent color
5. prop/icon motif

Text is optional and secondary.

---

## 5. Architecture

### Preferred

- layered building masses
- slightly imperfect symmetry
- visible roof structures
- readable entrances
- architectural depth
- facade variation
- material transitions
- buildings that relate to terrain

### Avoid

- pure front elevation repeated across a row
- identical widths/heights
- mathematically even window grids everywhere
- perfectly straight facades with no local variation
- isolated object-like buildings floating on terrain
- architecture that looks designed in a vector icon editor

Project buildings should feel like they belong to their district before they read as portfolio buttons.

---

## 6. Composition

Use three-depth composition whenever practical:

1. foreground framing / terrain / vegetation
2. midground primary subject
3. farther secondary interest

A scene should usually contain:

- a destination
- a curiosity point
- some hidden continuation

Do not show every route to its endpoint.

Roads should:

- bend
- climb
- descend
- pass behind buildings
- disappear through gates
- continue behind trees
- split and reconnect
- lead toward partially hidden landmarks

The city should reward looking around.

---

## 7. District composition

Do not arrange three project buildings as three equally sized front-facing cards.

Preferred relationships:

- one dominant / hero mass
- one side-facing or angled mass
- one deeper or partly hidden building
- varied elevation
- partial overlap
- road or terrain separation

Landmarks may be partially occluded.

Occlusion is useful when it increases spatial depth without making interaction ambiguous.

Important clickable silhouettes must still remain sufficiently readable for hotspot registration.

---

## 8. World Map composition

The World Map is one continuous illustrated geography, not a visible grid.

The internal Master Grid must not shape visitor-facing composition.

Use:

- irregular mainland silhouette
- asymmetric coastline
- hills/highlands
- water/inlet rhythm
- paths that ignore cell boundaries
- atmospheric frontier
- partially visible distant geography

Published districts should feel embedded in a larger world.

Do not make current districts read as four evenly spaced map icons.

---

## 9. Color language

Base palette:

- low-to-mid saturation
- warm natural neutrals
- aged painted surfaces
- controlled accent colors

Recommended visual balance:

- ~70% low-saturation environment/base
- ~20% medium-saturation district identity
- ~10% strong accent

This is a composition guide, not a literal pixel-count rule.

### Good base families

- warm cream
- muted sandstone
- pale grey
- desaturated brick
- dusty green
- blue-green
- muted ochre
- aged teal
- soft sky blue
- weathered red

### Strong accents

Reserve for:

- roofs
- awnings
- doors
- landmark ornaments
- project motifs
- small signs
- water sparkle
- brass/metal accents

Do not make the entire scene brown.

"Old" does not mean "sepia filter."

---

## 10. Lighting

Default:

- clear daytime
- soft warm-neutral sunlight
- shadows slightly cool
- no hard cinematic contrast
- shaded facades retain color

Lighting should support readability and age.

Avoid:

- PBR-style dramatic reflections
- hyper-realistic HDR contrast
- heavy bloom
- neon lighting unless a project specifically requires it
- each district having an incompatible time of day

District identity should come primarily from place design, not incompatible lighting conditions.

---

## 11. Lines and edges

Edges should feel drawn/painted rather than CAD-perfect.

Use:

- slight line-weight variation
- broken or softened secondary edges
- stronger contours only where silhouette clarity needs them
- painted material boundaries

Avoid:

- uniform thick black outlines
- vector-clean geometry everywhere
- razor-perfect repeated lines
- every small detail outlined equally

Line strength should follow importance.

---

## 12. Detail hierarchy

Do not distribute detail evenly.

### Highest detail

- main landmark
- current project buildings
- focal entrances
- immediate foreground
- distinctive props

### Medium detail

- neighboring facades
- trees near the focal area
- bridges
- walls
- street furniture

### Low detail

- far buildings
- distant hills
- background vegetation
- frontier terrain
- atmospheric distance

Distance should reduce:

- contrast
- saturation
- edge sharpness
- micro-detail

This selective detail is essential.

A scene with equal detail everywhere becomes noisy and loses storybook depth.

---

## 13. Nature

Nature should not look generated from geometric stamps.

Trees, shrubs, cliffs, and coastlines should vary in:

- silhouette
- mass
- density
- overlap
- scale

Foliage should read first as grouped masses, then selected detail.

Avoid:

- identical trees
- equally spaced bushes
- perfectly smooth coastlines
- uniform grass texture
- repeated rock shapes

Nature should soften architecture and support composition.

---

## 14. Roads and exploration

Roads are narrative devices.

A road should often create a question:

- where does that bend go?
- what is behind that wall?
- what is beyond the gate?
- where does that staircase lead?
- what is on the other side of the bridge?

Avoid long straight roads that expose the whole scene at once.

Use:

- turns
- elevation changes
- arches
- walls
- vegetation
- building corners
- partial cover

to stage discovery.

---

## 15. Atmosphere

Target emotional register:

- bright
- calm
- lived-in
- slightly old
- lightly mysterious
- not ominous
- not sterile

The scene should feel pleasant to inhabit while retaining a small amount of unanswered space.

Too cheerful and polished becomes theme-park glossy.

Too dark becomes gothic or oppressive.

The target sits between those extremes.

---

## 16. People and figures

People are optional and subordinate.

When present:

- use silhouette
- clothing
- posture
- grouping

more than detailed facial rendering.

Faces may remain simplified.

People should establish scale and life, not become the main visual subject unless a specific page requires it.

---

## 17. Signs and text

Do not depend on generated text inside art.

Prefer:

- symbolic motifs
- architectural signage shapes
- icons
- colors
- objects

Actual project names belong primarily in web UI / metadata.

If text exists inside an image, it should be:

- decorative
- non-essential
- short
- replaceable if needed

The interaction layer provides authoritative labels.

---

## 18. Project-building identity

A project building should be identifiable without reading its title.

Use one dominant idea.

Examples of identity mechanisms:

- Aquarium: watery/glass/marine silhouette
- HoloScope: observation/telescope/tower vocabulary
- Sphere: dome/celestial-globe vocabulary
- workshop projects: exposed craft structure / tools / materials
- archive projects: books / stacks / civic archive massing

Do not add five competing motifs to one building.

One strong motif plus supporting architecture is preferable.

---

## 19. Single-Scene production rule

For District Views, preferred production is now:

> **compose the finished townscape as one scene first**

Do not assume each clickable building must be independently rendered.

Benefits:

- one camera
- one perspective
- one lighting condition
- one material treatment
- one color grade
- one atmospheric pass

Interaction is added later with semantic hotspot geometry.

### Important

The art must still preserve readable interactive shapes.

Avoid completely burying project buildings behind foreground objects.

Partial occlusion is good.

Loss of semantic readability is not.

---

## 20. Future-growth strategy

A complete district may contain more architecture than currently active projects.

Future capacity can be represented as:

- ordinary baked-in facades
- civic buildings
- workshops
- houses
- unused but visually complete structures

They do **not** need to appear as empty lots or placeholder slots.

A future project may:

- claim an existing compatible building semantically
- trigger a new district edition
- introduce a newly integrated building through controlled repaint/edit

Do not damage the current composition merely to advertise empty future capacity.

---

## 21. World frontier

Frontier art should remain less committed than published areas.

Use:

- haze
- cloud
- sea mist
- softened land silhouettes
- lower detail
- lower contrast
- partial geography

Do not define speculative future districts too precisely.

Visible terrain does not automatically mean published district.

---

## 22. Image-generation / art-production language

When using generative tools or image-edit workflows, use the project's own style description.

Preferred prompt vocabulary:

- fictional European storybook town
- handcrafted illustrated architecture
- controlled irregularity
- aged painted colors
- warm muted materials
- selective detail
- readable building silhouettes
- curved exploratory streets
- layered depth
- softened distant scenery
- cohesive daylight
- integrated townscape
- lightly mysterious calm atmosphere

Avoid naming a proprietary franchise as the target style.

Use references for composition, architecture, palette, and production needs, not for copying distinctive protected characters, logos, landmarks, or UI.

---

## 23. Originality rule

Portfolio Storybook City Style is an original project grammar.

It may use broad traditions such as:

- European storybook illustration
- hand-painted town maps
- classical architecture
- theatrical perspective
- children's book environmental art
- old-town visual language

It must not copy:

- proprietary characters
- franchise-specific landmark designs
- unique logos
- recognizable UI frames
- exact compositions
- signature props tied to another IP

Borrow visual principles, not identity.

---

## 24. Review checklist

For every World / District candidate, review:

### Shape
- Are major buildings recognizable in silhouette?
- Is there enough height/roof/massing variation?
- Are repeated elements too regular?

### Perspective
- Do all major forms belong to the same camera?
- Does terrain support the buildings naturally?

### Color
- Is the base muted enough?
- Are accents localized?
- Has the image drifted into brown/sepia?

### Detail
- Is detail concentrated around focal areas?
- Is distance simplified?
- Is there unnecessary texture noise?

### Composition
- Is there a destination?
- Is there a secondary curiosity point?
- Is some route or space withheld from immediate view?

### Atmosphere
- Is it bright without becoming glossy?
- Is there age without dirtiness?
- Is there quiet mystery without darkness?

### Runtime suitability
- Are interactive silhouettes readable?
- Can hotspot geometry be registered confidently?
- Does the image still read at actual browser scale?

---

## 25. Strong rejection patterns

Reject or revise when the image shows:

- isometric buildings all at the same angle and scale
- perfectly equidistant windows
- perfectly repeated streetlights
- identical trees
- same texture density everywhere
- strong photorealistic PBR lighting
- glossy game-engine materials
- perfectly straight uniform roads
- rigid historical reconstruction
- all-brown / all-sepia palette
- all buildings at similar height
- every route shown to its endpoint
- empty space filled only because it exists
- three project buildings presented like front-facing cards
- isolated cutout buildings that do not share scene perspective/light
- vector-like building assets pasted over painted scenery
- generated text used as essential navigation
- cloud/frontier edges aligned to the logical grid

## 27. Canonical one-paragraph definition

Portfolio City is a fictional European storybook world built from mixed classical urban influences rather than strict historical reconstruction. Its imagery uses controlled irregularity, aged low-to-mid-saturation color, strong architectural silhouettes, selective detail, softened distance, and exploratory compositions that hide part of the route ahead. Buildings and landscapes feel hand-authored rather than mechanically perfect. Scenes remain bright and welcoming while preserving quiet age and a small sense of mystery. Visual coherence, place memory, and exploration take priority over photorealism or rigid architectural accuracy.

---

## 28. Acceptance statement

A successful Portfolio Storybook City image should make the viewer feel:

> "この場所、もう少し見て回りたい"

before they think:

> "これはWebサイトのナビゲーションだ"

The interface meaning is added by the semantic layer.

The illustration should first succeed as a place.
